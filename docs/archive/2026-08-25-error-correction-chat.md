user: yes i do want to ask this: So I was messing around with conlangs and there's this thing around error correction or not using the full space of syllables or not packing words into the phonetic, into like the most efficient Zipfian phonetic inventory. and natural languages have a certain ratio of things and when I was talking with Claude about this it was like, oh we'll use like half the space to get distance one away from things or something what I want to ask you is optimal is how optimal are existing distributions of syllable or phoneme use or saturation for natural languages and If they are optimal, then is there a way to get that distribution without... or basically to engineer that density without waiting hundreds of years for it to shake out? Or if it's not optimal, and we can ignore existing one-offs or something. I'm sure every language has their mistakes. But if it's severely not optimal, I kind of want to know why.

claude: Short version: natural languages are close to a Pareto frontier, but the objective function isn't the one a coding theorist would write down. Judged as block codes (maximize minimum distance in phonetic space) they look sloppy. Judged as joint source-channel codes decoded by a Bayesian listener with enormous contextual priors, they're pretty good. Most of the "waste" you're seeing is redundancy deliberately located somewhere other than the word form.

Evidence on the "surprisingly optimal" side:
There's a well-known 2019 result (Coupé, Pellegrino and colleagues) showing that across ~17 typologically diverse languages, information rate converges around 39 bits/second. Languages with low per-syllable information density (Japanese) are spoken fast; dense ones (Vietnamese, Mandarin) are spoken slow. That convergence smells like a channel-capacity constraint being saturated, which is exactly what you'd expect from an optimized system. Piantadosi, Tily and Gibson showed word length tracks average surprisal better than raw frequency, i.e. the lexicon approximates optimal source coding against contextual predictability, not just Zipf's law of abbreviation. Vowel inventories match dispersion-theoretic predictions (Liljencrants and Lindblom) closely: given N vowels, natural systems place them roughly where a perceptual-distance maximizer would. And my favorite for your purposes: Wedel and colleagues showed phoneme mergers over historical time are inhibited in proportion to how many minimal pairs the merger would collapse. That's literally error-correction pressure operating diachronically, and it's measurable. Phonotactics itself is a soft code constraint: the legal-syllable space is a sparse subset of possible phone sequences, so illegal sequences signal transmission errors and listeners snap to the nearest legal form. That's your check-bit scheme, just probabilistic instead of algebraic.

Evidence on the "not optimal in the way you'd expect" side, which is the interesting part:
Lexicons cluster in phonological space more than chance, not less (Dautriche, Mahowald and colleagues). High-frequency words sit in dense neighborhoods, are short, and are phonotactically bland. That's the opposite of distance-maximizing code design. The resolution is that for frequent, predictable words, context does the error correction almost for free, so spending form-level distance on them is wasted redundancy. Meanwhile clustering buys real things: articulatory routines get reused (production cost), phonotactically probable forms are learned faster (acquisition cost), and dense neighborhoods are actually easier to produce even though harder to perceive in isolation. Natural language allocates code distance where context is weak and tolerates near-collisions where context is strong. Homophones survive precisely when their contexts don't overlap.

So the answer to "if it's severely not optimal, why": it isn't severely suboptimal, it's optimizing a multi-term objective (articulation effort + learnability + memory + robustness) with a decoder that has priors your Hamming-distance intuition doesn't credit. The genuine inefficiencies are local historical debris (English spelling-tier stuff at the phonology level: mergers that overshot, morphological fossils), not the overall distributional shape.

For engineering it without the centuries: yes, absolutely, because diachrony is just a slow annealer for an objective you can write down explicitly. The recipe is roughly what you're already circling with confusion-weighted check bits:
1. Get the channel's actual confusion matrix (Miller-Nicely style perceptual data for speech; key adjacency and chord-slip statistics for your chorded input, which is a genuinely different noise model and probably more symmetric than human audition).
2. Estimate the frequency and contextual-predictability distribution of your meaning space by borrowing from corpora, since you can't wait for usage to reveal it.
3. Assign forms by solving the allocation problem: short, cheap, dense-neighborhood forms to frequent and contextually predictable meanings; long, high-distance forms to low-context, high-stakes items. The NATO alphabet ("niner") is the degenerate case of this done for a zero-context channel.

The one thing to hold in mind: the natural-language operating point assumes a fluent community with strong priors. A young conlang has no such decoder. So early on you should sit closer to the algebraic, high-distance end than natural languages do, and treat density as something you can migrate toward as fluency builds, the same way abbreviations only work once a jargon community exists. Explicit check bits also degrade less gracefully than distributed phonotactic redundancy but are verifiable, which for a designed language is probably the right trade.

If you want a concrete target ratio: Shannon-style estimates put English text redundancy around 50 to 75 percent. Your "half the space for distance" instinct lands you in that same neighborhood, which I'd read as convergent validation rather than coincidence.

user: "Explicit check bits also degrade less gracefully" - possible to engineer something that degrades more gracefully? check bit verifiability - any alternatives / approaches that are almost as good?

claude: Two separate problems hiding in that sentence, so let me split them: graceful degradation is about what happens when noise exceeds design distance; verifiability is about the listener knowing an error occurred. Explicit check bits are brittle on the first mostly because of how they're decoded, not what they are.

Making explicit checks degrade gracefully
The brittleness comes from hard-decision decoding: check matches or it doesn't, and past the design distance an algebraic code fails catastrophically by decoding confidently to the wrong codeword. Three fixes, all standard in coding theory and all portable to a conlang:
1. Soft-decision: the check mismatch is evidence, not a gate. The listener's decode rule is "nearest plausible word weighted by how badly the check fails," not "reject if check fails." This alone is worth roughly 2 dB in engineered systems, and it's free in your design since the decoder is a human who was going to do Bayesian inference anyway. You just have to not teach the language as if checks were binary.
2. Gray-map the check. You already have confusion-weighted check bits; go one step further and choose the check-symbol assignment so that likely confusions of content symbols produce likely confusions of the check symbol. Then a near-miss in the stem yields a near-miss in the check, distances add coherently, and the code behaves algebraically at low noise but soft and smooth at high noise. This is the single highest-leverage tweak to what you have.
3. LDPC-ify: replace one strong global check with many weak overlapping local ones. This is exactly why phonotactics degrades gracefully, since it is a sparse graph of local constraints decoded by message passing. The engineered linguistic versions are things natural languages already invented: vowel harmony (every vowel redundantly encodes a word-level feature, so the "check" is smeared across the whole word), fixed prosodic templates (CV skeleton, mora count, tone melody as independent soft channels), and cross-word agreement (gender or class concord is parity distributed over the phrase, and each agreement site is an independent piece of evidence). A word that carries harmony class plus a template plus agreement has no single point of failure; a word with one check syllable does.

Also worth stating the unequal-error-protection option, which is graceful degradation in meaning space rather than form space: put the coarse semantic layer in the perceptually privileged, high-distance position (word onsets, which get the best perception and drive incremental recognition) and let later material refine. Under heavy noise you lose precision, not identity, like hierarchical modulation in broadcast TV. Related trick: systematicity, where similar forms map to similar meanings, makes residual errors small in semantic terms. But it has a sharp edge the maritime world found empirically: starboard/larboard were confusable forms with opposite meanings in identical contexts, which is the worst possible configuration, and larboard got replaced by "port" for exactly that reason. Rule of thumb: neighbors in form space may share a semantic field only if confusing them is cheap or context disambiguates; never put antonyms or same-slot contrasts (left/right, on/off, digits) near each other.

Verifiability without explicit checks
The key reframe: a CRC is also only probabilistically verifiable (CRC-32 misses one error in 2^32), so "explicit check = verifiable, distributed = vibes" is a difference of degree, and you can hit any detection probability you want with distributed structure:

Lexical sparsity is an implicit checksum. If your lexicon occupies 1/8 of the phonotactically legal space, a random corruption lands on a nonword, and is therefore detected, about 87% of the time, better if you additionally keep the distance-1 neighborhoods of real words empty, which is your half-the-space scheme. The nice property is that this is tunable and quantifiable at design time: pick your saturation ratio, get your detection rate.

Template invariants give you human-computable verification, which is the real constraint you're up against: humans cannot compute a checksum function in real time, so an explicit check symbol is either memorized per word (at which point it's just extra codeword length, fine but not a "check") or it must be a function simple enough to evaluate while listening. The functions that qualify are global invariants: every word has even mora count, every word's tone melody comes from a licensed set determined by its first vowel, every content word fits one of four templates. Violation is instantly perceptible without computation, the same way a native English speaker detects "bnick" pre-lexically.

Agreement is cross-word parity and catches the errors word-internal checks can't: a corruption that turns one valid word into another valid word is invisible to any word-level scheme but trips a concord mismatch. Your mode particles are sitting right there; make them agree with some feature of the clause content and they become utterance-level check symbols for free.

And then the piece that makes detection almost as good as correction: ARQ. Human conversation already runs hybrid ARQ, weak forward error correction from phonotactics and context, plus a repair channel ("huh?", "which one?") that converts any detected error into a correction at the cost of a round trip. Dingemanse and Enfield's work suggests "huh?" is near-universal across languages, which tells you how load-bearing the feedback channel is. So for interactive use, detection plus cheap standardized repair initiators is genuinely near-optimal, and you should design the repair moves explicitly: a minimal "repeat from X" particle, and a canonical citation form of every word (long, maximally dispersed) distinct from the colloquial form (short, dense). That citation/reduced distinction is rateless coding by register shift, the same thing "V as in Victor" does, and it's the system-level graceful degradation: redundancy scales with observed channel state instead of being fixed at design time.

Last note on your specific stack: the chorded keyboard channel and the speech channel deserve different codes. Chord-slip noise is machine-observable, so let the IME carry true algebraic verification (it can compute real CRCs and squiggle-underline in real time; humans never see the math), and design the chord-to-grapheme map so common slips land on either illegal graphemes (detected) or semantically adjacent words (graceful). Reserve the distributed soft machinery, harmony, templates, agreement, sparsity, for the acoustic channel where the decoder is wetware. Matching code style to channel is not a compromise, it's just the correct engineering, and it lets you keep the verifiability you like without paying its brittleness tax where it hurts.
